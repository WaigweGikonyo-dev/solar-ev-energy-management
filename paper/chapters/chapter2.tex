\section{Literature Review}

\subsection{EV Charging in Weak Grid Systems}

Electric vehicle (EV) charging infrastructure is traditionally designed for stable, high-capacity electrical grids. However, in many developing regions, including Kenya and other Sub-Saharan African countries, distribution networks are characterized by limited transformer capacity, voltage fluctuations, and localized outages \cite{africa_ev_grid}.

These constraints significantly affect the scalability of EV charging systems. High-power charging loads introduce additional stress on already constrained feeders, leading to peak demand amplification and potential system instability. As a result, unmanaged charging strategies are unsuitable in weak-grid environments, necessitating coordinated energy management approaches.

---

\subsection{Solar PV Integration in EV Charging Systems}

Solar photovoltaic (PV) energy has emerged as a key renewable solution for EV charging due to its abundance and declining cost. In equatorial regions such as Kenya, high solar irradiance levels make PV systems technically viable for distributed charging infrastructure \cite{pv_ev_charging_review}.

However, PV generation is inherently intermittent and non-dispatchable. Its output varies with weather conditions, time of day, and seasonal changes. This intermittency introduces uncertainty in energy availability, making standalone PV systems insufficient for reliable EV charging without complementary energy buffering mechanisms.

---

\subsection{Battery Energy Storage in Hybrid Systems}

Battery Energy Storage Systems (BESS) are widely used to address the intermittency of renewable energy sources in hybrid systems. In EV charging applications, BESS provides multiple operational benefits, including peak shaving, load shifting, and smoothing of PV output fluctuations \cite{bess_hybrid_systems}.

Despite these advantages, battery systems introduce additional complexity. Improper sizing or inefficient energy management can lead to accelerated degradation, reduced lifecycle performance, and suboptimal utilization of stored energy. This makes the design of intelligent dispatch strategies critical in hybrid EV charging systems.

---

\subsection{Diesel Backup and Emissions Challenges}

In weak-grid environments, diesel generators are commonly deployed as backup power sources to ensure uninterrupted service delivery. While this approach improves system reliability, it introduces significant operational and environmental drawbacks.

Diesel generators contribute to high fuel consumption costs and increased greenhouse gas emissions, directly conflicting with the sustainability objectives of EV adoption \cite{diesel_microgrid_emissions}. Furthermore, reliance on fossil-fuel backup systems creates a hybrid dependency that reduces the overall environmental benefit of renewable integration.

---

\subsection{Energy Management Strategies in Hybrid Systems}

Energy management in hybrid renewable systems has been extensively studied and can generally be classified into three categories: rule-based methods, optimization-based approaches, and intelligent control systems.

Rule-based strategies are simple to implement but lack adaptability to dynamic conditions such as fluctuating demand and renewable variability. Optimization-based methods improve performance by minimizing cost or maximizing efficiency; however, they often rely on deterministic assumptions about system behavior.

More recent approaches utilize intelligent control techniques, including machine learning and predictive optimization. While these methods offer improved adaptability, they often require extensive data, high computational resources, and may lack interpretability for real-world deployment in resource-constrained environments \cite{energy_management_ev}.

---

\subsection{Research Gap and Motivation}

Despite significant progress in hybrid EV charging system design, several critical gaps remain unresolved.

First, most existing studies do not jointly optimize energy flow across solar PV, battery storage, grid supply, and diesel backup under realistic weak-grid conditions. Instead, they typically focus on partial system optimization.

Second, carbon emissions and fuel consumption are often treated as secondary metrics rather than primary optimization objectives. This limits the environmental relevance of proposed solutions, especially in fossil-fuel-dependent backup scenarios.

Third, many models assume ideal or highly stable grid conditions, which do not reflect the operational realities of Kenyan and similar Sub-Saharan African power systems.

Finally, there is a lack of lightweight, adaptive energy management strategies that can be realistically deployed in developing regions with limited computational infrastructure.

Therefore, this study proposes the development of an adaptive energy management system that simultaneously considers energy reliability, fuel consumption, and carbon emissions under Kenyan weak-grid conditions. The system is intended to bridge the gap between theoretical optimization models and practical deployable solutions for hybrid EV charging infrastructure.
